# Comprehensive Legal and Administrative Framework of Nepali Citizenship: An Exhaustive Analysis and Source Inventory

The legal and administrative framework governing Nepali citizenship represents a highly complex, historically layered intersection of constitutional mandates, statutory law, executive directives, and transformative judicial precedents. Following the promulgation of the Constitution of Nepal in 2015 (2072 BS), the state has undertaken significant legislative and administrative reforms designed to align its citizenship distribution mechanisms with constitutional guarantees of equality, non-discrimination, and fundamental human rights. This report provides an exhaustive examination of the existing citizenship landscape in Nepal. It systematically tracks the evolution of primary legislation, details the procedural mechanics enforced by District Administration Offices (DAOs) and Area Administration Offices (AAOs), analyzes the ongoing integration of citizenship data into the biometric National Identity (eNID) system, and unpacks the jurisprudence of the Supreme Court of Nepal that has actively dismantled patriarchal administrative legacies regarding matrilineal citizenship rights.


## Constitutional Underpinnings and Legislative Evolution

The foundation of modern Nepali citizenship law is unequivocally anchored in Part 2 of the Constitution of Nepal (2072 BS), encompassing Articles 10 through 151. The constitutional framing departs from previous historical texts by explicitly guaranteeing, under Article 10, that no Nepali citizen shall be deprived of the right to obtain citizenship, establishing the provision for a single federal citizenship accompanied by provincial identity2.

The Constitution delineates multiple, distinct pathways to citizenship. Article 11 establishes the parameters for citizenship by descent, acknowledging the rights of individuals whose father or mother is a Nepali citizen at the time of their birth2. Furthermore, it addresses the transition of individuals whose parents acquired citizenship by birth under previous regimes, guaranteeing their offspring the right to citizenship by descent2. Article 12 introduces a progressive mandate, allowing individuals acquiring citizenship by descent to obtain their certificates with explicit gender identity and lineage markers derived from either the mother or the father2. Article 14 introduces the entirely new category of Non-Resident Nepali (NRN) citizenship, designed to confer economic, social, and cultural rights—absent political rights—upon individuals of Nepali origin residing outside the South Asian Association for Regional Cooperation (SAARC) region2.

To operationalize these broad constitutional principles, the primary statutory instrument remains the Nepal Citizenship Act, 2063, originally published on 10 Mangsir 20634. However, the transition from the Interim Constitution of 2006 to the 2015 Constitution created profound statutory gaps, necessitating a series of highly contested and politically sensitive amendments to the Act.

## Administrative Directives and the Architecture of Local Service Delivery

The translation of citizenship jurisprudence into administrative reality is governed primarily by the Citizenship Certificate Distribution Directive, 2063, alongside the Citizenship Regulations15. This directive establishes the standardized workflows, required evidentiary burdens, and authority delegation models for Chief District Officers (CDOs), Assistant CDOs, and local ward secretaries.


## The Role of District Administration Offices (DAOs)

District Administration Offices (DAOs) function as the central nervous system of the state's citizenship distribution apparatus. An exhaustive analysis of the Citizen Charters (नागरिक बडापत्र) and official notices from DAOs across Kathmandu, Lalitpur, Bhaktapur, Kaski, Morang, and Panchthar reveals a highly formalized, evidence-heavy procedural standard17. The administrative burden rests heavily on the applicant to prove lineage and continuous residency.

The application process is entirely dependent on a localized recommendation system. Before an applicant can approach the DAO, they must secure a formal recommendation from their respective local ward office18.

The rigorous nature of these requirements is evident in the specific directives issued by DAOs following legislative amendments. For example, following the enactment of the First Amendment Act, DAO Kathmandu issued a dedicated administrative circular on 10 Ashadh 2080 (2080-03-10)10. This notice explicitly instructed all local wards to meticulously verify that both parents of the applicant are Nepali citizens and have continuously resided within the borders of Nepal before recommending citizenship by descent for the children of citizens by birth10. The circular mandated extensive secondary evidence to corroborate these claims, including land ownership documents (जग्गाधनी दर्ता प्रमाण पुर्जा), approved house blueprints, relationship verification certificates with photographs, and equivalence certificates for any foreign academic degrees10. Furthermore, DAO Kathmandu noted that it reserves the right to conduct on-site police inquiry reports (Sarjamin Muchulka) to verify the veracity of the applications10.

The procedural nuances regarding duplicate certificates also reveal the historical vulnerabilities of the state's record-keeping. DAO Kaski’s Citizen Charter explicitly notes that because its office building burned down, destroying official records prior to 2047-01-17 BS, any individual seeking a duplicate certificate for a document issued before that date must undergo the entire rigorous process required for issuing a brand-new citizenship certificate23.


## Decentralized Service Delivery: Area Administration Offices (AAOs)

To alleviate the administrative bottleneck at district headquarters and serve populations in geographically expansive or remote districts, the Ministry of Home Affairs relies on Area Administration Offices (AAOs). AAOs, such as those in Tikapur (Kailali), Pharping (Kathmandu), Burtiwang (Baglung), and Tharmare, possess delegated authority from the CDO to distribute citizenship and manage vital records20.

The official portal of AAO Tikapur explicitly lists fifteen core administrative functions29. Prominent among these are the distribution of citizenship by descent, the issuance of citizenship to the families of civil servants deployed in the area, and the issuance of duplicate certificates29. Crucially, AAO Tikapur is tasked with the correction and verification of past roster records29. During the mass citizenship distribution campaigns (mobile teams) of 2063 BS, numerous clerical errors occurred regarding names, birth dates, and addresses. AAOs hold the authority to investigate these historical records, verify evidence, correct minor errors, and issue updated citizenship certificates, subject to periodic endorsement by the CDO29. Furthermore, modern AAOs like Pharping utilize the centralized Citizenship Management Information System (CMIS) to digitally track recommendations, verify cross-district migrations, and coordinate with the central DAO apparatus20.


## Non-Resident Nepali (NRN) Citizenship Operationalization

The introduction of the NRN citizenship category via the First Amendment Act of 2079 necessitated a completely new administrative workflow, which is heavily detailed in the charters of DAOs like Lalitpur and Morang25. NRN citizenship represents a delicate constitutional compromise: granting economic, social, and cultural rights to the diaspora without violating the strict prohibition against dual political citizenship.

According to DAO Lalitpur’s specific NRN notice, the process requires the applicant to formally and legally relinquish their prior Nepali citizenship25. This is executed using Schedule-925. Applicants must present their foreign passport and foreign citizenship certificate, proving they are citizens of a non-SAARC nation25. Furthermore, they must sign a binding oath of allegiance to the Constitution of Nepal under Schedule-825. The administrative security protocols require that a close blood relative (who is a Nepali citizen) must be physically present at the DAO for Sanakhat (verification)25. If a close relative is unavailable, the DAO holds the authority to mandate a local police inquiry (Sarjamin Muchulka) or rely on verification by other recognized Nepali citizens25.


## Digital Integration: The National Identity (eNID) Ecosystem

The traditional, paper-based Nepali citizenship certificate is currently undergoing a massive structural transition. It is being systematically integrated into a centralized, biometric digital ecosystem managed by the Department of National ID and Civil Registration (DoNIDCR)31. The National Identity Card (eNID) acts as a unified repository of a citizen's vital events, biometric data, and citizenship status, intended to eventually replace the paper citizenship certificate for day-to-day service delivery, banking, and property transactions33.

This transition is governed by the National Identity Card and Civil Registration Act, 2076, and the corresponding Regulation of 2077 (subsequently amended in 2078)32. The eNID system is intrinsically reliant on the citizenship certificate, treating it as the foundational "breeder document." The DoNIDCR’s official guidelines stipulate that to enroll for an eNID, an individual must present their original Nepali citizenship certificate33.

However, this reliance on legacy paper documents creates significant data migration challenges. Many older citizenship certificates lack precise dates of birth, recording only the individual's age at the time of issuance33. The DoNIDCR has established protocols to resolve these discrepancies: if the exact date of birth is missing from the citizenship certificate, the system algorithms calculate the birth year based on the issuance date, or the enrollment officers rely on supplementary official documents—such as academic transcripts, passports, or pension records—to establish a definitive date of birth for the digital registry33.

A more profound administrative challenge involved the digital integration of citizens who acquired their citizenship solely through their mother's name. Historically, state administrative software, database architectures, and physical ledger books defaulted to patriarchal data fields, requiring a father's name as a mandatory primary key3. As the Supreme Court forced the state to issue matrilineal citizenship, the DoNIDCR had to update its backend systems. To formalize this, DoNIDCR issued a critical circular on 26 Falgun 2082 (2082-11-26) regarding the eNID data registration of Nepali citizens holding citizenship certificates issued under the mother's name35. This circular serves as a binding directive to all biometric enrollment stations at DAOs and local wards, ensuring that the software permits enrollment without the father's citizenship details, thereby removing a significant procedural friction point that previously hindered matrilineal citizens from accessing digital state services35.


## Jurisprudential Paradigm Shift: Dismantling Patriarchal Administration

The most dynamic and consequential aspect of modern Nepali citizenship law has not originated from the legislature, but from the Supreme Court of Nepal. Despite explicit constitutional guarantees in Article 11(2) and Article 38(1) ensuring equality and equal lineage rights, deeply entrenched patriarchal administrative practices at the DAO level frequently obstructed the issuance of citizenship through the mother2. DAOs routinely rejected applications if the father was unidentified, foreign, or had abandoned the family, demanding the father's citizenship certificate as an absolute prerequisite. It required a series of landmark, binding interventions by the Supreme Court to force the executive branch to comply with the Constitution.

The judiciary’s foundational stance was established in the case of Sabina Damai v. Government of Nepal (NKP 2068, Decision No. 8557)36. In this case, a child was born and raised by a single Nepali mother who could not identify the father36. The DAO refused citizenship. The Supreme Court ruled that denying citizenship to a child raised by a Nepali mother simply because the father was unidentified directly violated the state's obligations under the Constitution and international human rights instruments, specifically the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination Against Women (CEDAW)36. The Court established that an individual cannot be rendered stateless due to the circumstances of their birth, paving the way for matrilineal citizenship rights36.

This principle was heavily expanded and solidified in Sajda Sapkota v. Government of Nepal (NKP 2073, Decision No. 9627)37. A joint bench consisting of then-Chief Justice Kalyan Shrestha and Justice Om Prakash Mishra reviewed a scenario where a father abandoned his family shortly after his children's birth37. The mother, a citizen by descent, fully raised and educated the children in Kathmandu37. When the children applied for citizenship, DAO Kathmandu refused to register the applications, review the documents, or issue the certificates, orally claiming citizenship could not be granted without the father's details37. The Supreme Court delivered a scathing rebuke of the administration. It ruled that when the law establishes that a person born to a Nepali mother can obtain citizenship, making eligible persons stateless by raising unwarranted doubts about the absent father's nationality is unlawful37. The Court issued a writ of mandamus to immediately grant the citizenship and, critically, issued a directive order to the Ministry of Home Affairs to establish a grievance-handling mechanism to ensure citizens applying through their mothers do not face systemic harassment37.

The administrative fixation on the father's documentation was further dismantled in Srijan Kharel v. Government of Nepal (NKP 2073, Decision No. 9687)38. The petitioner's father had absconded to avoid legal responsibilities, and DAO Kathmandu rejected the petitioner's citizenship application under the mother's name, strictly demanding the father's citizenship certificate38. Justices Dr. Ananda Mohan Bhattarai and Prakash Man Singh Raut held that when an applicant explicitly requests citizenship under the mother's name—and the mother's citizenship is undisputed—the whereabouts, status, or identity of the father are legally irrelevant to the process38. The Court established a critical administrative principle: citizenship is a substantive right tied to personal identity. When substantive eligibility is proven (i.e., the mother is a citizen), denying citizenship based on procedural technicalities or minor evidentiary hurdles violates natural justice and the constitutional right to equality38.

Perhaps the most comprehensive and binding articulation of this legal doctrine occurred in Uma Bhattarai and Sandesh Singh v. District Administration Office, Morang (NKP 2074, Decision No. 9841)3. In this instance, a mother (a citizen by descent) raised three children after her husband went missing for 17 years3. The local ward conducted a thorough field inquiry (Sarjamin Muchulka) confirming the facts and recommending citizenship, yet the DAO obstructed the process3. Justice Sapna Pradhan Malla, writing for the joint bench, invoked Articles 7 and 8 of the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child, noting that the state has an absolute, non-derogable duty to prevent statelessness and protect a child's right to a nationality3.

The Court provided a definitive interpretation of the law: Section 3(1) of the Nepal Citizenship Act and Article 11(2) of the Constitution use the disjunctive conjunction "or" (father or mother)3. Therefore, any administrative action that discriminates against a mother's ability to independently pass on citizenship constitutes a direct violation of women's equal lineage rights guaranteed under Article 38(1), and the equality clause under Article 183. Recognizing that DAOs were repeatedly ignoring previous precedents, the Supreme Court issued a mandatory, sweeping directive to the Ministry of Home Affairs3. It ordered the Ministry to send mandatory instructions to all DAOs nationwide, prohibiting them from denying or impeding citizenship in the mother's name due to a patriarchal mindset or treating women as secondary, dependent citizens3. The Court mandated that strict disciplinary action must be taken against any authority violating this directive3.
